# Verified facts sheet

Facts a writer may state in the books. Checked in November 2026 against official sources or
multiple reliable secondary sources. **Always pair each with "confirm the current rule at
[source]"** — rates and rules change. If a fact you want is not here, describe the concept
without the specific number and send the reader to the official source.

## GST/HST (Canada Revenue Agency — canada.ca/revenue-agency)

- GST is 5% federally. Five provinces use HST (combined federal + provincial): Ontario 13%;
  New Brunswick, Newfoundland and Labrador, and Prince Edward Island 15%; **Nova Scotia 14%**
  (lowered from 15% on April 1, 2025). Other provinces/territories: 5% GST, plus a separate
  provincial sales tax in some (BC, Saskatchewan, Manitoba PST; Quebec QST). Say: "check CRA's
  current GST/HST rates page before you set up your invoices."
- The rate you charge generally depends on where the supply is made (place-of-supply rules),
  not where your business is. For services, this is often where the customer is. Keep this
  general; send the reader to CRA or an accountant for edge cases.
- **Small supplier:** you generally do not have to register if your total taxable revenues
  (with associated businesses) are $30,000 or less over the last four consecutive calendar
  quarters *and* in any single calendar quarter. (Taxi/rideshare drivers and some others must
  register regardless.) Voluntary registration is allowed.
  - Four-quarter test: if you go over $30,000 across four quarters, you stop being a small
    supplier at the end of the month after the quarter in which you went over. Start charging
    at the beginning of the following month.
  - Single-quarter test: if you go over $30,000 in a single calendar quarter, you stop being a
    small supplier on the sale that put you over, and you charge tax on that sale.
  - You must register within 29 days of making your first taxable sale as a non-small supplier.
- **Input tax credits (ITCs):** registrants can generally claim back the GST/HST they paid on
  business purchases, if they have the required documentation.
- **ITC documentation tiers** (Input Tax Credit Information Regulations, updated 2021):
  the information an invoice/receipt must show depends on the total: under $100; $100 to under
  $500; $500 or more. More details are required at each tier (for example, the supplier's
  GST/HST registration number from the $100 tier; buyer's name, payment terms and a description
  at the $500 tier). Tell readers to check CRA's guidance for the exact list.
- A GST/HST number is the 9-digit Business Number plus the program identifier RT and a
  4-digit reference (e.g., 123456789 RT0001). Use obviously fake example numbers.
- Reporting periods are monthly, quarterly, or annual; the default assigned depends on revenue.
  Annual filers may have to pay instalments. Don't state the dollar thresholds — send to CRA.
- The Quick Method of accounting exists for eligible small businesses (remit a set percentage
  of tax-included revenue). Do not state the percentages; send to CRA.
- **Record keeping:** CRA generally requires you to keep books and records for six years from
  the end of the last tax year they relate to. Electronic records are acceptable; CRA has
  rules for keeping electronic records and for imaging (scanning) paper records, and the
  records must be readable and available on request. Tell readers to check CRA's guidance on
  keeping records before they shred originals.

## Invoices and late payment

- Interest Act (Canada), section 4: when a written agreement states interest as a rate per
  day/week/month or other period less than a year, it must also state the equivalent yearly
  rate, or interest is generally limited to 5% per year. Practical takeaway: if your terms say
  "2% per month", also say "(24% per year)". Whether and how this applies to a particular
  invoice is a question for a lawyer — say so.
- Late fees/interest generally need to be agreed in advance (in the quote, contract, or terms
  the customer accepted), not just added to an invoice after the fact. Present this as good
  practice and "ask a lawyer", not as a legal ruling.
- Small claims court exists in every province with its own monetary limit and process. Do not
  state limits; say "check your province's small claims court website".

## Hiring a first employee (federal pieces apply everywhere)

- Open a payroll program account (RP) with CRA before the first pay day.
- Get the employee's Social Insurance Number (SIN) and completed TD1 forms (federal and
  provincial/territorial).
- Deduct CPP contributions (in Quebec, QPP), EI premiums, and income tax; pay the employer
  share of CPP and EI; remit to CRA on the schedule CRA assigns. Since 2024 there is also a
  second additional CPP contribution (CPP2) on earnings above the first ceiling. Don't state
  rates or ceilings — they change every year; point to CRA's payroll deductions calculator
  (PDOC).
- Issue a T4 slip for each employee and file the T4 information return by the last day of
  February following the calendar year.
- Issue a Record of Employment (ROE) through Service Canada when an employee has an
  interruption of earnings (e.g., they quit, are laid off, or go on leave).
- Employment standards (minimum wage, hours, overtime, vacation, statutory holidays,
  termination notice) are **provincial** for most businesses. In Nova Scotia these are in the
  Labour Standards Code (novascotia.ca). Don't state the minimum wage figure; say it changes
  and to check the province's site. Nova Scotia general rule: vacation pay of at least 4% of
  wages (rising after years of service) — prefer "check the Labour Standards Code" over
  quoting the service thresholds.
- Employee vs contractor: CRA looks at the whole working relationship (control, tools,
  chance of profit/risk of loss, integration). Getting it wrong can mean owing unremitted
  deductions. CRA can make a ruling (CPP/EI ruling) if unsure.
- Workers' compensation is provincial (WCB Nova Scotia, WSIB Ontario, WorkSafeNB, WorkSafeBC,
  etc.).
- Privacy: collect only the personal information you need and store it securely.

## WCB Nova Scotia (wcb.ns.ca)

- WCB Nova Scotia provides no-fault workplace injury insurance for employers and workers in
  the province. In exchange, covered workers generally cannot sue their employer for a
  workplace injury.
- Coverage is mandatory for most businesses in industries listed as mandatory once they have
  **three or more workers at the same time**. Register within **10 days** of meeting those
  conditions. Businesses with two or fewer workers are generally not required to register,
  but can apply for voluntary coverage. Some industries are not covered by mandatory rules —
  check WCB's list of mandatory industries. Edge cases (part-time, seasonal, family members,
  owners/directors) → call WCB.
- Out-of-province firms: generally need coverage if they have three or more workers in Nova
  Scotia for five or more days in a year.
- Owners/proprietors/partners are generally not automatically covered as workers; personal
  coverage for owners can be purchased. Confirm with WCB.
- Premiums ("assessments") are based on the employer's industry classification (rate group)
  and assessable payroll, per $100 of assessable payroll, up to a yearly maximum assessable
  earnings figure per worker. Experience rating can adjust an employer's rate. Do not state
  rates or the maximum — they change yearly; send to WCB.
- Employers report payroll (estimates and actuals) to WCB; MyAccount is WCB's online portal.
- **Injury reporting:** employers must report a workplace injury that results in lost time
  beyond the day of injury or needs medical treatment beyond first aid. WCB's current guidance
  (MyAccount forms page) says to report **within two business days** of becoming aware of the
  injury (this was shortened from five business days; WCB's older pages may still say five).
  Say: "At the time of writing WCB Nova Scotia says two business days — confirm on wcb.ns.ca."
  Reports are filed through MyAccount or by the WCB Injury Report form.
- Return to work: employers and workers have duties to co-operate in early and safe return to
  work. Keep it general.
- Workplace safety itself (OHS) is regulated by the province under the Occupational Health and
  Safety Act (Nova Scotia), enforced by the provincial department, not WCB. Many NS employers
  with fewer than 20 employees still have safety duties (policy requirements scale with size).
  Keep this general and point to novascotia.ca.
- Hiring contractors: in Nova Scotia you can ask a contractor for proof they are in good
  standing with WCB (WCB offers a way to check/confirm an account's status — describe as
  "ask for proof of WCB good standing and check with WCB"). If an uncovered contractor's
  workers are hurt on your job, you may be responsible for their coverage — confirm with WCB.
- WCB Nova Scotia also runs safety and prevention programs (e.g., WorkSafe for Life). Mention
  generally.

## Google Business Profile (support.google.com/business)

- Free listing that can appear in Google Search and Google Maps.
- Must be verified before it is fully public/editable; verification methods vary (video,
  phone, email, mail, etc.) and Google decides which are offered.
- Service-area businesses (that go to customers) can hide their address and list a service area.
- Google's guidelines: use your real-world business name (no keyword stuffing), accurate
  category, real address, no fake reviews, no offering incentives for reviews, no
  "review gating" (only asking happy customers). Breaking guidelines can lead to suspension.
- Owners can reply to reviews publicly. Owners cannot delete reviews; they can report reviews
  that break Google's policies.
- Google ended Business Profile websites (the free sites made from profiles) in 2024 and
  ended Business Profile chat in 2024. Don't recommend those features.
- Do not promise rankings. Google says local results are based mainly on relevance, distance,
  and prominence — that is Google's own framing and can be cited as such.

## Reviews (Competition Bureau Canada — competition-bureau.canada.ca)

- The Competition Act prohibits false or misleading representations. The Competition Bureau
  has warned that fake reviews, reviews by owners/employees posing as customers, and hiding
  negative reviews can be misleading. If you offer an incentive for a review on your own
  channels, the incentive should be disclosed. Platform rules can be stricter (Google does not
  allow incentivized reviews at all).
- Privacy: don't reveal a customer's personal information in a public reply. Private-sector
  privacy law (PIPEDA federally; Alberta, BC and Quebec have their own) applies to personal
  information collected in commercial activity.
- Defamation is a legal question; if a review is threatening, defamatory, or reveals private
  information, report it and consider a lawyer. Never threaten a reviewer.

## Booking systems and paperless

- Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation (CASL) applies to commercial electronic messages
  (marketing emails/texts). Transactional messages such as appointment confirmations and
  reminders about a service the customer already booked are generally treated differently from
  marketing, but promotional messages need consent. Keep it general: "get consent before
  sending marketing messages; ask a lawyer if unsure."
- PIPEDA (or the provincial equivalent) applies to customer personal information: collect
  what you need, protect it, tell people what you do with it. When you use cloud software, you
  remain responsible for the information.
- Deposits/cancellation fees: state them clearly before booking and get agreement; rules on
  deposits and consumer contracts vary by province (consumer protection laws). Send to the
  province.
- Electronic signatures are generally recognized in Canada under federal and provincial
  electronic commerce laws, with exceptions (e.g., some documents like wills). Keep general.
- CRA accepts electronic records (see record keeping above).
